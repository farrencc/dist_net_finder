TITLE
  Every mode but one decays; the slowest sets the clock

SOURCE
  Notebook : graph-workshop/notebooks/session2_instructor.ipynb
  Cell     : 22
  Section  : 3 - Diffusion (clock 0:45)
  Image    : 06-modal-amplitude-decay.png

WHAT IT SHOWS
  A log-log plot with time on the x axis (about 0.2 to 130) and modal amplitude
  |c_k * exp(-lambda_k * t)| on the y axis (1e-6 to 1).

  Many pale grey curves - all the higher modes - fall off a cliff early, most of
  them dropping below 1e-6 within the first decade of time. A thick crimson
  curve, the lambda_2 mode, stays almost flat through the early period and only
  bends down late. A black dashed horizontal line at about 0.078 is the
  lambda_1 = 0 mode: the conserved mean, which never decays at all. A dotted
  vertical line marks t = 1/lambda_2.

NUMBERS ON THE FIGURE
  lambda_2 = 0.0609, so tau = 1/lambda_2 is about 16.4.
  lambda_1 = 0: the conserved mean (flat dashed line).

WHY IT IS HERE
  The explanation for everything in this section. Write the initial condition in
  the eigenvector basis and each component simply decays as exp(-lambda_k * t).
  The lambda_1 = 0 mode never decays, which is conservation of total mass. Every
  other mode dies, and it dies at a rate set by its own eigenvalue.

  So after a short transient, only one non-trivial mode is left with any
  amplitude: the lambda_2 mode. Its shape is the Fiedler vector. That is why the
  late-time diffusion pattern looks like the spectral partition - not by
  analogy, but because by then it literally is the Fiedler vector, scaled.

  It also justifies the choice of measurement time throughout the capstone.
  t = 1/lambda_2 is the moment when the slow mode is still alive and everything
  faster has already gone.
