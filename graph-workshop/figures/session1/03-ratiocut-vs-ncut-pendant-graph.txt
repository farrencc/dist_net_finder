WHAT IT SHOWS
  Two panels sharing a y axis, for a graph built as a clique of 12 and a clique
  of 4 joined by three bridge edges, plus one pendant node hanging off the end.
  The x axis is the node index 0-16; the y axis is the Fiedler entry, rescaled
  so the largest magnitude is 1. Blue circles are the clique of 12, orange
  squares the clique of 4, and a large crimson star is the pendant node.

  Left, "RatioCut relaxation: eigh(L)": the pendant star is pinned at +1.0,
  far above everything else. The clique of 12 sits in a tight band near +0.1
  to +0.2 and the clique of 4 near -0.6 to -0.8. The eigenvector's whole
  dynamic range is spent separating one lonely node from the other sixteen.

  Right, "Ncut relaxation: eigh(L, D)": the pendant star has dropped to about
  +0.15, back inside the range of the clique of 12. The dominant contrast is
  now between the two cliques, with the clique of 4 pushed out to -0.8 to
  -1.0.

NUMBERS ON THE FIGURE

WHY IT IS HERE
  RatioCut balances a partition by counting nodes, so slicing off a single
  pendant node is a cheap cut and the unnormalised eigenvector goes chasing it.
  Ncut balances by volume (degree sum), so a degree-1 node carries almost no
  weight and stops dominating.

  This is the concrete reason scikit-learn, and most practice, uses the
  normalised Laplacian: real graphs are full of low-degree stragglers, and an
  algorithm that keeps proposing to cut them off is useless. The generalised
  eigenproblem eigh(L, D) is the entire fix.
